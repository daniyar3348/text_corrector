# Turan: Detecting and Correcting Kazakh Spelling Errors, with a Focus on the "ka2ru" Cyrillic Look-Alike Phenomenon

## Abstract

A pervasive but under-studied source of Kazakh spelling errors arises when the nine Kazakh-specific Cyrillic letters (қ, ә, ң, ұ, ү, ғ, і, ө, һ) are typed as visually similar standard-Russian Cyrillic letters because writers lack or ignore the Kazakh keyboard layout. We term this look-alike substitution the *ka2ru* error class. Because the corrupted text remains Cyrillic and superficially resembles Russian, it also defeats naive language identification. We address this problem with a two-stage pipeline that separates error detection from correction. A KazRoBERTa [liu2019roberta; kztransformers2023kazroberta] encoder is fine-tuned as a token classifier that labels each token KEEP or ERROR, following the tag-not-rewrite paradigm for grammatical error correction [omelianchuk2020gector]; flagged tokens are then resolved by a corrector that combines inverse look-alike substitutions, a frequency-weighted lexicon, restricted Damerau–Levenshtein candidate search [damerau1964technique], and morphological guarding of valid inflected forms. To train and evaluate the system, we construct a parallel corpus of 2,615,771 sentence pairs (a training split of 2,565,771 pairs) by injecting six error types into cleaned Kazakh text via confusion-set-based synthetic generation [grundkiewicz2019synthetic], with *ka2ru* as the dominant class (30.5% of injected edits). On a held-out test set the detector attains a token-level F1 of 97.3% (precision 97.1%, recall 97.4%), and the full pipeline corrects 80.5% of sentences completely, approaching an oracle ceiling of 83.5% obtained under perfect detection. On this synthetic distribution these results indicate that detection is essentially solved and that the corrector is the remaining bottleneck. The system runs CPU-only and was validated in an accepted live pilot. We discuss the principal limitation candidly: the evaluation is synthetic and in-distribution with respect to the generator, and a human-annotated benchmark, competitive baselines, and component ablations remain future work.

## Keywords

Kazakh spelling correction; *ka2ru* look-alike errors; KazRoBERTa; token classification; synthetic error generation; language identification; agglutinative morphology

## 1. Introduction

Kazakh is a Turkic language written in a Cyrillic script and is, by the standards of contemporary natural language processing (NLP), a low-resource language: large annotated corpora, gold-standard error-correction datasets, and mature downstream tooling remain comparatively scarce. It is also strongly agglutinative, forming words by concatenating long sequences of suffixes that encode number, possession, case, and other categories onto a stem under vowel harmony. The combination of rich productive morphology and limited supervision makes Kazakh text processing difficult: the effective vocabulary is enormous, surface forms proliferate, and fixed lexicons cover only a fraction of the inflected forms that occur in practice. Subword tokenization mitigates the vocabulary explosion [sennrich2016bpe], and Transformer encoders pretrained with masked language modeling have become the standard backbone for sequence-labeling tasks in such settings [vaswani2017attention; devlin2019bert; liu2019roberta]. Dedicated monolingual models tend to outperform multilingual ones on individual languages, motivating Kazakh-specific encoders rather than reliance on multilingual checkpoints [pires2019multilingual]. KazRoBERTa, a RoBERTa-style encoder pretrained on Kazakh, is one such model and is now publicly available [kztransformers2023kazroberta; sagyndyk2025kazroberta].

A specific and widespread source of textual noise in Kazakh writing motivates this work. The Kazakh Cyrillic alphabet extends the Russian one with nine letters that have no Russian counterpart: қ, ә, ң, ұ, ү, ғ, і, ө, and һ. Because users frequently lack a Kazakh keyboard layout, or ignore it for convenience, they substitute the visually similar standard-Russian Cyrillic letters: қ→к, ә→а, ң→н, ұ→у, ү→у, ғ→г, і→и, ө→о, and һ→х. We refer to this error class as *ka2ru*. The result is still Cyrillic text, but it is no longer correct Kazakh: special letters are systematically lost, and the surface form frequently resembles broken Russian. This phenomenon harms downstream NLP in two distinct ways. First, the words themselves are corrupted, degrading any task that depends on correct orthography, from lexical lookup to search and indexing. Second, and more insidiously, it breaks language identification: because the discriminative Kazakh letters are exactly the ones removed, a *ka2ru*-corrupted Kazakh sentence can be misclassified as Russian, causing it to be routed to the wrong processing path entirely. The error is therefore not merely cosmetic; it propagates into and corrupts pipeline-level decisions.

Despite its prevalence, this exact phenomenon has received little focused attention at scale. Prior Kazakh language technology has emphasized foundational resources — corpora [makhambetov2013kazcorpus], named-entity datasets [yeshpanov2022kaznerd], rule-based and finite-state morphological analyzers [kessikbayeva2014kazakh; washington2014kypchak], machine translation [salimzyanov2013kazakhtatar], and treebanks [tyers2015kazakhud] — while recent hybrid systems combine finite-state methods, conditional random fields, and Transformer encoders for Kazakh text correction [baitenova2025hybrid]. General spelling correction has a long history of nonword detection and isolated correction [kukich1992techniques], with edit-distance models underpinning candidate ranking [levenshtein1966binary; damerau1964technique], and modern grammatical error correction increasingly frames the task as edit tagging over a pretrained encoder rather than full rewriting [awasthi2019pie; omelianchuk2020gector], often trained on synthetically generated error pairs [grundkiewicz2019synthetic]. What is largely missing is a system that targets the *ka2ru* look-alike substitution specifically, at corpus scale, and that addresses both its orthographic and its language-routing consequences.

This paper presents *Turan Text Corrector*, an automatic spelling- and typo-correction system for Kazakh built around the *ka2ru* phenomenon. We construct a reproducible cleaning pipeline and a synthetic-error generator over a large historical-text corpus, producing a parallel dataset whose training split contains 2,565,771 corrupted–correct pairs (and 2,615,771 pairs in total across train, validation, and test); the dominant injected error class is *ka2ru*, accounting for 30.5% of all edits. The system itself is a two-stage pipeline: a KazRoBERTa token-classification detector that labels each token KEEP or ERROR, feeding a corrector that combines inverse look-alike substitutions, a frequency-weighted lexicon, restricted Damerau–Levenshtein (Optimal String Alignment) candidate search, and morphological guarding of valid inflected forms. To address misrouting, we introduce a lightweight "russified-lexicon" language-routing signal that maps the Kazakh lexicon through the *ka2ru* substitution and uses length-aware, nonlinear, Russian-stopword-excluded coverage to recover special-letter-stripped Kazakh that would otherwise be read as Russian. On the held-out test set, the detector attains an F1 of 97.3% (precision 97.1%, recall 97.4%), and the full pipeline corrects 80.5% of sentences end to end — within roughly three points of an 83.5% oracle ceiling obtained with perfect detection — substantially exceeding a heuristic baseline (64.6% end to end). The system runs on CPU at a warm latency of about 9 ms per request and was validated in an accepted live pilot.

We are explicit at the outset about the scope of these claims. All reported numbers are computed on a synthetic test set whose errors were generated by the same confusion maps that the corrector partially inverts; the figures are therefore in-distribution with respect to the generator and should be read as upper-leaning estimates of organic performance. The strengths we claim are the problem framing, the engineering of a deployable system, and the careful data construction; we treat a gold human-annotated benchmark, competitive learned baselines, component ablations, and a quantitative evaluation of the language router as necessary future work (Sections 8 and 9). The principal contributions of this work are:

- **A reproducible cleaning and synthetic-error-generation methodology** for Kazakh spelling correction, yielding a large parallel dataset (training split of 2,565,771 pairs; 2,615,771 pairs in total) centered on the under-studied *ka2ru* look-alike phenomenon, with explicit edit metadata and an identity-pair fraction of 10.86% to suppress over-correction.
- **A two-stage detection-then-correction pipeline** pairing a KazRoBERTa token-classification error detector (test F1 = 97.3%) with a lexicon- and edit-distance-based corrector that uses morphological guarding, reaching 80.5% end-to-end sentence-level correction — within about three points of the 83.5% oracle ceiling — and indicating that, on this synthetic distribution, detection is essentially solved while the corrector is the remaining bottleneck.
- **A lightweight "russified-lexicon" language-routing signal** that maps the Kazakh lexicon through the *ka2ru* substitution and applies nonlinear, length-aware, Russian-stopword-excluded scoring, directly addressing the language-identification failure mode that the *ka2ru* error class creates. We present this as an engineering mechanism; it is not yet quantitatively benchmarked, and we mark such an evaluation as future work.
- **A deployable, CPU-only system** with a throughput ceiling of approximately 200 requests per second, a running-container footprint of about 373 MB of RAM, and a 2.84 GB linux/arm64 image, validated in an accepted live pilot.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Transformer Encoders and RoBERTa

The Transformer architecture [vaswani2017attention] introduced fully attention-based sequence modelling and is the foundation of modern pretrained encoders. BERT [devlin2019bert] established the masked-language-modelling (MLM) paradigm, in which a bidirectional encoder is pretrained on large unlabelled corpora and subsequently fine-tuned for downstream tasks, including per-token labelling. RoBERTa [liu2019roberta] refined this recipe through longer training, larger batches, dynamic masking, and the removal of the next-sentence-prediction objective, yielding consistently stronger representations. A practical prerequisite for applying these models to morphologically rich languages is subword tokenization; byte-pair encoding [sennrich2016bpe] segments words into reusable units and mitigates the out-of-vocabulary problem that an agglutinative language such as Kazakh would otherwise present. Our detector builds directly on this lineage: it fine-tunes KazRoBERTa [kztransformers2023kazroberta; sagyndyk2025kazroberta], a RoBERTa-style encoder pretrained with MLM on Kazakh text using a byte-level BPE vocabulary, as a token-classification model that emits a binary KEEP/ERROR label per token.

### 2.2 Kazakh, Turkic, and Low-Resource NLP and Morphology

Kazakh is an agglutinative Turkic language whose inflection is governed by vowel harmony (сингармонизм), making morphological well-formedness a useful signal for distinguishing genuine words from corrupted ones. Foundational language resources include the Kazakh Language Corpus [makhambetov2013kazcorpus], which provides large-scale clean text, and the Kazakh Universal Dependencies treebank [tyers2015kazakhud], which adds syntactic annotation. Token-level supervised tasks for Kazakh have been formalized through datasets such as KazNERD [yeshpanov2022kaznerd], establishing sequence-labelling as a viable setup for the language and motivating our framing of error detection as token classification. Rule-based morphological processing for Kazakh and related Kypchak languages has a substantial open-source tradition: two-level and finite-state analyzers [kessikbayeva2014kazakh; washington2014kypchak] segment a surface form into a stem and ordered NUMBER/POSS/CASE suffixes and verify harmony, and broader rule-based pipelines have been developed for Kazakh–Tatar machine translation [salimzyanov2013kazakhtatar]. Because dedicated monolingual encoders tend to outperform multilingual models on cross-lingual transfer for individual languages [pires2019multilingual], we adopt a Kazakh-specific encoder; we note that this is an a priori design choice rather than an empirically demonstrated superiority for the present task, since we do not yet compare against a multilingual encoder (Section 8). Our system reuses the morphological-analysis idea as a *guard*: a lightweight analyzer that segments stem and suffixes and validates vowel harmony is used to leave well-formed inflected forms untouched, rather than as a standalone correction engine.

### 2.3 Grammatical Error Detection, Correction, and Spelling Correction

Automatic correction of written text has been studied since early work on isolated-word and context-dependent spelling correction; Kukich [kukich1992techniques] surveys the classical decomposition into non-word detection and candidate ranking that still structures the field. More recent grammatical error correction (GEC) has converged on neural sequence-labelling rather than full sequence-to-sequence rewriting. Parallel Iterative Edit models [awasthi2019pie] cast correction as predicting local edit tags over a BERT encoder, and GECToR [omelianchuk2020gector] demonstrated that a Transformer tagger ("tag, not rewrite") is both fast and accurate. Our pipeline shares this token-classification philosophy but deliberately separates the two sub-problems: a learned detector identifies *where* errors occur, while a deterministic corrector decides *how* to fix them. The most directly comparable Kazakh-specific work is the hybrid architecture of Baitenova et al. [baitenova2025hybrid], which combines finite-state transducers, conditional random fields, and KazRoBERTa/mBERT for Kazakh text correction. We differ in scope and emphasis: we target spelling and typing errors centred on a specific, under-studied phenomenon — the *ka2ru* class, in which Kazakh-specific Cyrillic letters (қ ә ң ұ ү ғ і ө һ) are typed as visually similar standard-Russian Cyrillic letters because the Kazakh keyboard layout is unavailable or ignored — and we pair the detector with a transparent lexicon- and edit-distance-based corrector with morphological guarding. We note explicitly that we do not yet provide an empirical comparison against Baitenova et al., a sequence-to-sequence GEC model, or an off-the-shelf spellchecker; situating our results against such baselines is identified as future work (Section 8). We also note that we report a homegrown "fully corrected sentence" metric rather than the standard GEC metrics M2/ERRANT or GLEU; adopting a standard metric would aid comparability with the broader GEC literature and is left to future work.

### 2.4 Synthetic Error Generation and Data Augmentation for GEC

A persistent obstacle for GEC in any language is the scarcity of annotated parallel data, which is especially acute for Kazakh. A standard remedy is to inject artificial errors into clean text and train on the resulting synthetic pairs. Grundkiewicz et al. [grundkiewicz2019synthetic] showed that confusion-set-based synthetic corruption, followed by training on the generated pairs, substantially improves neural GEC. We adapt this strategy to Kazakh spelling correction: clean sentences derived from a cleaned historical-article corpus are corrupted by six error operations — *ka2ru* look-alike substitution, vowel-harmony violation, character swap, deletion, duplication, and substitution — to form a parallel dataset. The realized distribution is dominated by the *ka2ru* class (30.5% of injected edits), followed by harmony (21.3%) and swap (17.9%). The training split comprises 2,565,771 corrupted/correct pairs (the full dataset, including validation and test, totals 2,615,771 pairs), of which approximately 11% are identity (clean-to-clean) pairs included to suppress over-correction. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale synthetic dataset for Kazakh spelling correction organized around the *ka2ru* look-alike phenomenon. A known limitation, shared with synthetic-data approaches generally, is that programmatically injected errors approximate but do not equal the distribution of human-typed errors; no gold human-annotated Kazakh test set is yet available. A subtler consequence, which we make explicit in Section 8, is that our corrector inverts the very confusion maps used to inject the *ka2ru* and harmony errors, so the per-type results for those classes are partly in-distribution by construction.

### 2.5 Edit-Distance Methods

Candidate ranking in spelling correction has long relied on string-edit metrics. Levenshtein distance [levenshtein1966binary] counts the minimal insertions, deletions, and substitutions between two strings, while Damerau [damerau1964technique] additionally modelled adjacent-character transposition, capturing a large fraction of single-character human typing errors. Our corrector's fallback candidate search uses Optimal String Alignment distance — a restricted form of Damerau–Levenshtein with transposition cost 1 — over a frequency-weighted lexicon of 339,717 surface forms (174,100 with frequency ≥ 2), with a frequency tie-break. Rather than relying on edit distance alone, we first generate candidates by inverting the language-specific *ka2ru* and harmony substitutions and filtering by lexicon membership, falling back to edit-distance search only when needed. This combination of classical edit-distance ranking with language-specific confusion sets and morphological guarding is what differentiates our corrector from generic isolated-word spelling correctors.

## 3. Data and Error Modeling

This section describes the construction of the parallel corpus used to train and evaluate the system, proceeding from the raw source corpus through a deterministic cleaning pipeline, the error taxonomy, and the synthetic generation of correct–corrupted pairs. Because no gold-standard, human-annotated corpus of Kazakh spelling errors exists at the scale required for supervised training, we follow the standard practice of programmatically injecting errors into clean reference text [grundkiewicz2019synthetic]. The central modeling decision is to make the error distribution dominated by the *ka2ru* look-alike phenomenon, which prior Kazakh correction work has not modeled explicitly [baitenova2025hybrid].

### 3.1 Source Corpus

The raw material is a single merged JSON file (`merged_dissertation.json`) of 568,374,253 bytes (approximately 542 MiB) containing Kazakh-language historical articles harvested from the *ehistory* domain; the project work log records 93,218 articles (the article count is taken from the work log and not independently recounted from the JSON). The corpus is descriptive rather than prescriptive: it reflects published written Kazakh as it appears online, including occasional non-standard spellings. This property matters for interpreting later results, since attested non-normative forms are treated as valid by the lexicon and are therefore not auto-corrected. The *ehistory* collection was chosen for its topical coherence and the predominance of well-formed sentences.

### 3.2 Cleaning Pipeline

Raw web text is converted to a clean ground-truth set through a deterministic, reproducible pipeline (`kk_text.py`) applied uniformly to every article.

1. **Unicode normalization.** Text is normalized to NFC so that canonically equivalent code-point sequences are unified.
2. **Look-alike normalization.** A small mapping rewrites exactly two confusable letter pairs to their intended Kazakh code points: the Latin schwa (ə/Ə → ә/Ә) and the Serbian/Cyrillic tshe (ћ/Ћ → һ/Һ). This removes encoding-level confusables only and is distinct from the deliberately injected *ka2ru* errors discussed below.
3. **Invisible-character stripping.** Soft hyphens (U+00AD), zero-width and bidirectional control characters (U+200B–200F, U+202A–202E, U+2060–206F), Private Use Area code points (U+E000–F8FF), and the byte-order mark (U+FEFF) are removed, since these are invisible to authors yet perturb tokenization.
4. **Run-on segmentation.** A sentence-boundary fix splits run-on text at terminal punctuation; the rule requires at least two preceding lowercase letters before the mark so that initials and abbreviations (e.g. «т.б.») are not erroneously split. The work log reports that this reduces the run-on rate from approximately 9% to 0.04%.
5. **Foreign-script removal.** Tokens and bracketed groups in non-Cyrillic scripts (e.g. Latin insertions) are removed, while all Cyrillic content is retained.

After cleaning, the work log reports that approximately 92% of the resulting sentences satisfy Kazakh vowel harmony, which we take as corroboration that the cleaned text is suitable as clean ground truth. (Both the run-on and harmony figures are reported in the project log and are not recomputed here.) Following segmentation and exact-duplicate removal, the corpus contains 922,798 unique clean sentences (`sentences.txt`), the pool from which all training and evaluation pairs are drawn.

### 3.3 Error Taxonomy

Corruption is modeled as a composition of six elementary edit operations applied at the character or word level. Table 1 (dataset statistics) and Figure 2 (error-type distribution) summarize their realized frequencies, which differ from the sampling weights because operations apply to differing fractions of tokens.

- **ka2ru** (realized 30.5%, 1,157,438 edits). The flagship class: Kazakh-specific Cyrillic letters are replaced by visually similar standard-Russian Cyrillic letters, modeling authors who lack or ignore the Kazakh keyboard layout. The substitution map is {ә→а, ө→о, ұ→у, ү→у, қ→к, ғ→г, ң→н, і→и, һ→х}; for example, *қазақ* ("Kazakh") becomes *казак*. The output remains Cyrillic and superficially resembles broken Russian, which is precisely why it also disrupts language identification. It carries the highest sampling weight (0.45) and is the dominant error.
- **harmony** (realized 21.34%, 809,903 edits). A vowel-harmony (сингармонизм) violation in which a vowel is replaced by its non-harmonic counterpart from a back/front pair such as а↔ә or о↔ө; for example, *өте* ("very") becomes *оте*.
- **swap** (realized 17.93%, 680,471 edits). Transposition of two adjacent characters, e.g. *мектеп* ("school") → *мекетп*; this motivates the transposition-aware edit distance used in the corrector [damerau1964technique].
- **drop** (realized 11.91%, 451,816 edits). Deletion of a single character, e.g. *маңызды* ("important") → *маңзды*.
- **dup** (realized 9.64%, 366,027 edits). Duplication of a character, e.g. *болып* ("being") → *боллып*.
- **sub** (realized 8.68%, 329,509 edits). Substitution of a character by an arbitrary other, modeling generic keystroke errors, e.g. *тұр* → *тыр*.

These six operations cover the principal classes identified in the spelling-correction literature — insertion, deletion, substitution, and transposition [damerau1964technique; kukich1992techniques] — augmented by two phenomena specific to written Kazakh, namely *ka2ru* layout substitution and vowel-harmony violation. The emphasis on *ka2ru*, at 30.5% of all injected edits, distinguishes this dataset from generic confusion-set corpora.

### 3.4 Synthetic Pair Generation

Errors are injected into the clean sentences by `build_kazakh_dataset.py` to form parallel pairs. Each clean sentence is corrupted at a per-character error rate of 0.12, with at most 3 errors per sentence (`max_errors = 3`) and a fixed random seed (`seed = 42`) for reproducibility. Operations are selected according to the sampling weights [ka2ru 0.45, harmony 0.18, swap 0.13, drop 0.10, dup 0.07, sub 0.07], with the realized per-edit shares differing from these weights because operations apply to different fractions of candidate tokens. The training split is built with ×3 augmentation, generating three independently corrupted variants per source sentence so the model observes diverse corruptions of the same target.

To suppress over-correction — the tendency to alter already-correct words — a substantial fraction of pairs are *identity* (clean→clean) pairs in which the corrupted side equals the correct side. The realized clean ratio is 0.1086 (approximately 11%), corresponding to 284,038 pairs. These teach the detector to emit KEEP and the corrector to make no change when the input is already valid. Because identity pairs are trivially "fully corrected," their inclusion in the end-to-end denominator is a known confound; we flag this in Section 8 and identify reporting the metric with clean pairs excluded as future work.

Each pair is stored with the schema {`correct`, `corrupted`, `n_errors`, `clean`, `edits`}, where `clean` is a boolean identity flag and `edits` is a list of operation records of the form {`type`, `wi`, `before`, `after`} giving the operation type, the word index, and the pre- and post-edit surface forms. This explicit metadata enables exact-edit verification: every corrupted sentence can be reconstructed from its correct counterpart by replaying the recorded edits, and the builder verifies this reconstruction, guaranteeing that the stored edits are exact rather than approximate.

The data are partitioned into disjoint splits with no cross-split sentence leakage: a source sentence and all of its augmented variants reside entirely within a single split. Before augmentation, 50,000 of the 922,798 unique cleaned sentences are held out for evaluation (25,000 for validation and 25,000 for test), leaving 872,798 source sentences for the training split. The realized sizes are 2,565,771 training pairs (from those 872,798 source sentences under ×3 augmentation), 25,000 validation pairs, and 25,000 test pairs, for a combined total of 2,615,771 pairs; we report the training split as 2,565,771 and reserve 2,615,771 for the full dataset. We note that a naive 872,798 × 3 would give 2,618,394 pairs; the realized 2,565,771 is slightly smaller, the difference (≈52,600 pairs) arising from the generator's identity/duplicate handling rather than from cross-split leakage. The per-sentence error count is distributed as 0→284,038, 1→1,336,004, 2→528,027, and 3→467,702, giving an average of 1.451 errors per pair. Full statistics are reported in Table 1 and the error-type distribution in Figure 2.

We note one limitation of this construction, addressed more fully in Section 8: the errors are programmatically injected and therefore approximate, but do not exactly equal, the distribution produced by human typists. As no gold human-annotated Kazakh spelling-error test set is currently available, all reported metrics are computed against this synthetic distribution.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 Overview: a two-stage detect-then-correct pipeline

The proposed system corrects Kazakh spelling and typing errors with a two-stage pipeline that separates *where* an error occurs from *what* the corrected form should be. The first stage is a neural **error detector**: a KazRoBERTa [liu2019roberta; sagyndyk2025kazroberta] token-classification model that reads the input sentence and emits a per-token KEEP/ERROR judgement. The second stage is a **corrector** that, for every flagged token, generates candidates from a frequency lexicon using inverse look-alike substitutions and an optimal-string-alignment edit-distance fallback, while a morphological analyser guards valid inflected forms from spurious edits. The two stages communicate through a single interface — the set of token positions the detector flags — so the corrector acts only where indicated and otherwise leaves text untouched. Figure 1 depicts the data flow from raw input through language routing, detection, candidate generation, morphological guarding, and final reconstruction.

This factorisation is deliberate. Detection is a balanced binary sequence-labelling task that a pretrained encoder solves with high accuracy, whereas correction is a precision-sensitive lexical task where uncontrolled generation risks corrupting already-correct words. Restricting the generative component to detector-flagged positions and to in-lexicon candidates yields a conservative failure mode: when no confident candidate exists, the word is left unchanged rather than rewritten.

### 4.2 Error detector: KazRoBERTa token classification

The detector fine-tunes `kz-transformers/kaz-roberta-conversational` [kztransformers2023kazroberta; sagyndyk2025kazroberta], a RoBERTa-style Transformer encoder [vaswani2017attention; liu2019roberta] pretrained with masked-language modelling on Kazakh text and using a byte-level BPE vocabulary [sennrich2016bpe] of 52,000 subwords. The encoder has 6 layers, 12 attention heads, and hidden size 768 (a deliberately compact configuration, confirmed in `models/kazakh_detector/config.json`). A token-classification head with two labels, id2label = {0: KEEP, 1: ERROR}, is placed on top of the encoder [devlin2019bert].

Training labels are derived by whitespace alignment. The corrupted and the reference sentence are split on whitespace, and each word *aᵢ* in the corrupted sentence is compared against the aligned reference word *bᵢ*; the label is tag = 𝟙[*aᵢ* ≠ *bᵢ*]. Because corruption preserves the number of whitespace tokens, the two word lists stay aligned position by position. After subword tokenisation, only the first subword of each word carries the word-level label; continuation subwords and special tokens are set to the ignore index −100 and do not contribute to the loss. Approximately 10.7% of labelled tokens are ERROR [TODO: verify], giving a usefully balanced labelling task.

We adopt token classification rather than a sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) formulation for three reasons. First, the targeted errors are *local* edits — look-alike letter substitutions, vowel-harmony violations, single-character swaps, drops, duplications and substitutions — so a non-autoregressive edit-tagging view aligns naturally with the problem, as in edit-based grammatical error correction [awasthi2019pie; omelianchuk2020gector]. Second, a tagger factorises detection from generation, allowing a precise, lexicon-constrained corrector to handle rewriting deterministically; a seq2seq decoder would fold both into one model and could hallucinate substitutions on already-correct tokens. Third, the tagger is cheaper to train and serve and needs no autoregressive decoding at inference. We note that these are motivating arguments rather than an empirical demonstration; a head-to-head comparison against a seq2seq baseline is left to future work (Section 8). The model is fine-tuned for 2 epochs (≈ 80,182 optimiser steps) on a Colab T4 GPU using AdamW with learning rate 2×10⁻⁵, batch size 64, weight decay 0.01, maximum sequence length 128, and mixed-precision (fp16); the best checkpoint is selected by ERROR F1 on the validation split. The resulting checkpoint occupies 316.1 MiB (331,459,608 bytes) in `safetensors` format. On the held-out test set (8,000-sentence subset), the detector attains F1 = 97.3% (precision 97.1%, recall 97.4%), versus F1 = 86.3% for a heuristic baseline that flags any word absent from the lexicon (Table 2).

### 4.3 Corrector: lexicon-constrained candidates with an edit-distance fallback

Given the detector's flagged positions, the corrector replaces each Cyrillic substring of a flagged word while preserving surrounding punctuation, hyphenated parts and original casing. Two complementary candidate generators are used.

The first generator applies **inverse look-alike substitutions**. A bidirectional substitution map encodes the visually similar Russian↔Kazakh Cyrillic pairs and the vowel-harmony pairs — for example а↔ә, о↔ө, у→{ұ, ү}, к↔қ, г↔ғ, н↔ң, и→і, і→{ы, и}, х↔һ. The map directly inverts the two dominant error classes (the *ka2ru* look-alike class and vowel-harmony violations). To bound the combinatorial expansion, substitutions are enumerated over at most six replaceable positions, and only candidates that appear in the lexicon are retained. Among the surviving candidates the most frequent form is chosen.

The second generator is an **edit-distance fallback** for swap, drop, duplication and substitution errors that look-alike inversion cannot resolve. It searches the lexicon with `rapidfuzz` `process.extract` using the optimal-string-alignment (OSA) distance — a restricted Damerau–Levenshtein metric in which a transposition counts as a single edit (cost 1) [levenshtein1966binary; damerau1964technique; kukich1992techniques] — with a score cutoff of 2 edits. The search pool is limited to lexicon forms whose length is within the distance bound of the query. Among the minimum-distance forms the most frequent one is selected (frequency tie-break).

When the detector flags a word that is itself a valid lexicon entry but wrong in context (for example, the Russified surface form *казак* whose intended Kazakh form is *қазақ*), the corrector runs in a **force-correct** mode: it re-evaluates even in-lexicon words and accepts a candidate only if the candidate's frequency is at least that of the original surface form, avoiding replacements toward rarer words. The lexicon is built from the cleaned corpus by `build_lexicon.py` and contains 339,717 surface forms; the *valid* subset used for candidate filtering retains the 174,100 forms with frequency ≥ 2, which suppresses noise from corpus artefacts. (The lexicon is derived from the cleaned *ehistory* corpus described in Section 3, not from an external dictionary.)

The correction decision for a single word can be summarised as follows.

1. Lower-case the word; if the detector did **not** flag it and it is in the valid lexicon, return it unchanged.
2. Generate inverse-substitution candidates and keep those in the valid lexicon.
3. If any in-lexicon inverse candidates exist, choose the most frequent one; in force-correct mode, keep the original if it is valid and at least as frequent as the best candidate; otherwise apply the candidate (restoring original casing).
4. If no inverse candidate exists but the word is valid, return it unchanged.
5. Otherwise run the OSA edit-distance search (≤ 2 edits); return the nearest form (frequency tie-break), or the original word if no candidate is within the cutoff.

At oracle detection on the test set the corrector fixes 88% of erroneous words with 91.6% precision of changes — where precision of changes is defined as the number of applied changes that match the gold form divided by the total number of changes the corrector applies — with strong per-type performance on the dominant classes (*ka2ru* 97.6%, harmony 97.9%, duplication 93%) and weaker performance on transposition, substitution and deletion (swap 83%, sub 70%, drop 62%), reflecting the absence of contextual re-ranking (Table 4).

### 4.4 Morphological guarding

Kazakh is agglutinative, so a finite lexicon cannot enumerate every inflected word-form; without protection, the edit-distance fallback would "correct" valid inflections toward whatever dictionary word lies closest. To prevent this, a morphological analyser segments a surface form in the canonical nominal order STEM (+NUMBER) (+POSSESSIVE) (+CASE) [kessikbayeva2014kazakh; washington2014kypchak]. It strips suffixes from the right — case, then possessive, then number — using declared allomorph inventories, requires at least two stem characters to remain (avoiding degenerate single-letter stems), and reverses stem-final voicing alternations (қ→ғ, к→г, п→б) when recovering the dictionary stem. Each segmentation is checked for **vowel-harmony consistency**: every vowel in the stripped suffix string must belong to the stem's harmony class (back or front), rejecting an accidental suffix-shaped ending on a foreign or misspelled stem. A word is treated as a valid inflected form, and left untouched, only if it admits a segmentation whose stem is a known lexicon entry *and* whose suffixes are harmony-consistent. For example, *мектептердің* is recognised as *мектеп*-PL-GEN and is therefore not edit-distance "corrected". The guard currently covers nominal morphology only (number, possessive, case); the far richer Kazakh verbal morphology is not modelled, so valid verb forms are not protected by the guard and could in principle be altered by the edit-distance fallback. Quantifying that risk is left to future work (Section 8).

### 4.5 Language routing

Because the *ka2ru* error class strips the Kazakh-specific letters, corrupted Kazakh text remains Cyrillic but superficially resembles (broken) Russian, which misleads conventional language identification and, with multilingual models, the wrong-language routing this entails [pires2019multilingual]. The router adds a **russified-lexicon coverage** signal to counter this. The Kazakh valid lexicon is mapped through the *ka2ru* substitution {ә→а, ө→о, ұ→у, ү→у, қ→к, ғ→г, ң→н, і→и, һ→х} to build a set of *russified-Kazakh* forms (restricted to forms of length ≥ 3 and frequency ≥ 3). The signal kaz_lex_ratio is the fraction of input tokens that fall in this set **and** are not Russian stopwords, so genuinely Russian function words do not inflate the Kazakh score.

The signal is length-aware and nonlinear. For inputs of three or more tokens it ramps only above a coverage of one half, kaz_lex_signal = max(0, (kaz_lex_ratio − 0.5)/0.5), so a few coincidental matches do not flip routing. For one- or two-token inputs, where context is scarce, the signal is 1.0 only when *all* tokens are russified-Kazakh and 0.0 otherwise. The router combines this with character- and token-level cues, kaz_score = 0.6·kaz_char_ratio + 0.7·kaz_lex_signal + 0.1·kaz_token_ratio; the Russian branch is a deliberate no-op (no Russian model), avoiding corruption of valid Russian. For example, «Бұл мәселе өте маңызды болып тұр», corrupted to «Бул маселе оте манызды болып тур», is routed to Kazakh and restored to its canonical form.

We emphasise that the router's threshold and weight constants (the 0.5 ramp, the 0.6/0.7/0.1 weights, the length ≥ 3 and frequency ≥ 3 cut-offs) are hand-set and not tuned or validated against a labelled language-identification benchmark. We therefore present the router as an engineering mechanism that addresses a concrete failure mode, and we identify a quantitative evaluation against established language-ID baselines (e.g. fastText, CLD3, langid) as required future work (Section 8).

## 5. Experimental Setup

### 5.1 Training configuration

The error-detection stage is obtained by fine-tuning the pretrained KazRoBERTa encoder (`kz-transformers/kaz-roberta-conversational`) [kztransformers2023kazroberta; sagyndyk2025kazroberta] for token classification with a two-label head (KEEP = 0, ERROR = 1), instantiated through the `AutoModelForTokenClassification` interface. KazRoBERTa follows the RoBERTa pretraining recipe [liu2019roberta] over the Transformer encoder [vaswani2017attention; devlin2019bert] and uses a byte-level BPE vocabulary [sennrich2016bpe]. The final model reported in this paper was trained in a Colab GPU notebook; a separate local script exists only for smoke testing and is not used for the reported results.

All hyperparameters below are taken from the final-run configuration (Table 5). The encoder was fine-tuned with the AdamW optimizer (the Hugging Face `Trainer` default; no optimizer name is set explicitly), a peak learning rate of 2×10⁻⁵, and weight decay of 0.01. The per-device training and evaluation batch sizes were 64 and 128, respectively, and input sequences were truncated to a maximum length of 128 tokens. Training ran for 2 epochs, corresponding to 80,182 optimization steps over the 2,565,771-pair training split (consistent with 2,565,771 / 64 × 2). No learning-rate warmup was configured (default warmup_ratio = 0.0) and the learning rate followed the default linear decay schedule. Mixed-precision training was enabled in 16-bit floating point (`fp16 = True`; not bf16). The random seed was the framework default (42). Evaluation and checkpointing were performed once per epoch, with the best checkpoint (by ERROR-class F1) restored at the end of training. Gradient accumulation, gradient clipping, the scheduler type, and dropout were left at their framework defaults. The training hardware was a single Colab NVIDIA T4 GPU, and the resulting fine-tuned checkpoint is 316.1 MiB (331,459,608 bytes) in `safetensors` format. Per-token ERROR labels were derived by whitespace word alignment of the corrupted and reference sentences, labelling only the first sub-word of each word and assigning the ignore index (−100) to continuation sub-words and special tokens; the ERROR-token fraction in training is approximately 10.7% [TODO: verify].

We note that all reported figures come from a single training run with one seed and a single fixed evaluation subset; we report no confidence intervals or significance tests. Multiple-seed training and bootstrap confidence intervals on the sentence-level metric are identified as future work (Section 8).

The serving stack uses Python 3.13 and `torch==2.12.1+cpu`, as recorded in the project's dedicated machine-learning environment and the Docker build.

### 5.2 Evaluation protocol

We evaluate at two levels of granularity on the held-out test split, which provides 25,000 pairs disjoint from the training and validation splits. Token-level and end-to-end measurements are reported on a fixed 8,000-sentence subset of this test split. The subset was fixed in advance; reporting on the full 25,000-pair test set would strengthen the result and is left to future work [TODO: re-run evaluation on the full test split].

At the **token level**, the detector is assessed as a binary classifier and we report precision, recall, and F1 on the positive (ERROR) class, where a token counts as correct only if its predicted KEEP/ERROR label matches the gold label.

At the **sentence level**, we report an end-to-end "fully corrected" metric: the fraction of test sentences for which the complete two-stage pipeline reproduces the reference sentence exactly (every injected error corrected and no spurious change introduced). This stricter criterion penalizes both missed corrections and over-corrections, and is the primary measure of practical system utility. We note that the test subset includes identity (clean) pairs, which are trivially "fully corrected"; their inclusion inflates the absolute end-to-end percentages, and a clean-pair-excluded variant of the metric is left to future work. As an internal diagnostic of the correction stage in isolation, we additionally report, under the oracle-detection condition described below, the proportion of erroneous words successfully corrected and the precision of the changes the corrector applies (precision of changes = correctly-applied changes / total applied changes), broken down by error type (ka2ru, vowel-harmony, duplication, swap, substitution, deletion). All test errors are synthetically injected by the data-generation procedure, which approximates but does not equal the distribution of human-typed errors; the system has not yet been evaluated against a gold human-annotated test set (see Section 8).

### 5.3 Baselines

We compare the trained pipeline against two reference points that bound the achievable range.

The first is a **heuristic out-of-lexicon detector**: a non-learned baseline that flags a word as erroneous if and only if its surface form is absent from the Kazakh lexicon. Requiring no training, it isolates the contribution of the learned KazRoBERTa detector over a simple dictionary-lookup strategy and is evaluated under the same protocols as the trained system. We acknowledge that this is a weak baseline; stronger learned comparisons (a sequence-to-sequence GEC model, a multilingual encoder such as XLM-R, a direct comparison to Baitenova et al. [baitenova2025hybrid], and an off-the-shelf spellchecker) are not included here and are identified as future work (Section 8).

The second is an **oracle upper bound** corresponding to perfect detection. Here the corrector receives gold error positions (detection F1 = 100% by construction), so the sentence-level result reflects the ceiling imposed by the correction stage alone, independent of detector errors. Comparing the trained pipeline against this ceiling isolates the residual gap attributable to the corrector. The per-error-type corrector statistics are reported under this oracle-detection condition.

### 5.4 Implementation and serving environment

The system is implemented as a FastAPI service running in the project's dedicated machine-learning environment (Python 3.13 with PyTorch 2.12 [TODO: verify versions]). For latency-sensitive serving, the correction endpoints execute synchronously in the server thread pool, intra-op parallelism is constrained with `torch.set_num_threads(1)`, and the model is warmed up at process startup to avoid first-request initialization cost. The corrector relies on a frequency-weighted lexicon of 339,717 surface forms (174,100 retained as valid at frequency ≥ 2) and a `rapidfuzz`-backed Optimal String Alignment search (restricted Damerau–Levenshtein distance with unit transposition cost) [levenshtein1966binary; damerau1964technique], guarded by a rule-based morphological analyzer. The service is packaged as a 2.84 GB `linux/arm64` Docker image (`torch==2.12.1+cpu`); a running container occupies approximately 373 MB of RAM. On the CPU-only test host the warm per-request latency is approximately 9 ms, and a single worker sustains on the order of 150–193 requests/s, with throughput plateauing near 200 requests/s under multi-worker configurations. Detailed throughput and latency figures are reported in Table 6.

## 6. Results and Analysis

We evaluate the system along five axes: (i) error-detection quality against a lexicon heuristic; (ii) end-to-end sentence-level correction against a non-learned baseline and an oracle ceiling; (iii) a per-error-type decomposition of the corrector under oracle detection; (iv) qualitative behaviour; and (v) efficiency and deployment. All results are reported on the held-out test split (25,000 sentence pairs), with detection and end-to-end figures measured on an 8,000-sentence subset. The errors are synthetically injected according to the six-operation error model of Section 3, so the figures characterise behaviour on a programmatically generated rather than human-typed error distribution (see Section 8).

### 6.1 Detection Results

The KazRoBERTa token-classification detector attains a token-level F1 of 97.3% (precision 97.1%, recall 97.4%) on the test subset. As a non-learned reference point we evaluate a lexicon heuristic that flags any whitespace token absent from the corrector lexicon; this baseline reaches F1 = 86.3%. These figures are summarised in Table 2. (Per-detector false-positive counts referenced in earlier project documentation could not be located in any verifiable artefact and are therefore not reported here; obtaining exact counts by re-running the evaluation is noted as future work.)

The eleven-point F1 gap illustrates why a contextual encoder is preferable to a membership test. The heuristic cannot distinguish a genuinely misspelled token from a valid but low-frequency or out-of-vocabulary form (a rare proper noun, a neologism, or a legitimately inflected surface form), so it over-flags. The fine-tuned detector, building on the masked-language-model pretraining of KazRoBERTa [liu2019roberta; sagyndyk2025kazroberta] and byte-level subword tokenization for agglutinative Kazakh [sennrich2016bpe], conditions each token decision on sentential context and thereby suppresses the spurious flags that dominate the heuristic's error budget — consistent with the long-standing observation that dictionary-lookup detection is limited and that context is needed to separate true errors from rare valid words [kukich1992techniques].

At the sentence level — measuring the fraction of test sentences that are *fully* corrected, i.e. that exactly match the reference after correction — the full pipeline corrects 80.5% of sentences. First, replacing the heuristic detector with the trained detector improves full-sentence correction by 15.9 points (64.6% → 80.5%), confirming that detection quality is the dominant lever once the corrector is held fixed. Second, the trained pipeline sits within 3.0 points of the 83.5% oracle ceiling. Because the oracle differs from the trained pipeline only in receiving perfect KEEP/ERROR labels, this small residual is precisely the cost of the detector's remaining recall and precision shortfall. The interpretation is that, *on this synthetic error distribution*, detection is essentially solved: even a perfect detector would lift end-to-end accuracy by at most about three points. The remaining 16.5 points separating the oracle from a hypothetical perfect system are attributable to the corrector — the corrector, not the detector, is the binding constraint on overall quality. We stress that this conclusion is conditioned on the synthetic generator and the specific error rate (0.12, max 3 errors); on organic text with denser or differently distributed errors the detector's behaviour is unknown, and the claim should not be read as a general finding (Section 8). ### 6.3 Per-Error-Type Corrector Analysis (Oracle)

Under oracle detection the corrector fixes 88.0% of flagged words with a precision-of-changes of 91.6%. Disaggregating by error type reveals a strongly bimodal profile (Table 4): the corrector is near-perfect on the substitution-based classes — ka2ru 97.6% and vowel-harmony 97.9% — strong on character duplication (dup) at 93.0% and character swap at 83.0%, but markedly weaker on character substitution (sub) at 70.0% and character drop (deletion) at 62.0%.

The pattern follows from the corrector's candidate-generation mechanism and the information content of each corruption. The ka2ru and harmony classes are deterministic, *reversible* character-level substitutions over a small confusion set (the ka2ru look-alike map {ә→а, ө→о, ұ→у, ү→у, қ→к, ғ→г, ң→н, і→и, һ→х} and the harmony vowel pairs). No surface information is destroyed: every corrupted character still corresponds to its correct counterpart, so the inverse-substitution generator, filtered by lexicon membership, recovers the intended word almost without ambiguity. We caution that this near-perfect recovery is partly a property of the evaluation rather than of the world: the corrector inverts exactly the maps used to inject these two classes, so the 97.6%/97.9% figures are in-distribution by construction and should be expected to fall on organic, more varied corruptions (Section 8). Duplication is similarly low-entropy — a repeated character is locally redundant and easily removed by the edit-distance fallback (Optimal String Alignment, a restricted Damerau–Levenshtein metric with unit transposition cost [damerau1964technique; levenshtein1966binary]). A swap (transposition) is by construction a single OSA operation and so remains within reach, though its 83.0% rate already shows the corrector struggling when more than one valid lexicon form lies within the search radius.

Drop and sub are the hardest because each *destroys* information that the corrector cannot recover from form alone. A deletion removes a character outright: the corrupted token is shorter, and multiple distinct lexicon entries may be equidistant from it, so the frequency tie-break must guess among genuinely competing reconstructions. A substitution replaces a character with an arbitrary other letter rather than a predictable look-alike, breaking the inverse-substitution path entirely and pushing every such case into the generic edit-distance search, where — as with drop — several plausible candidates often coexist. Crucially, the corrector ranks candidates by lexicon frequency, not by sentential plausibility: it has no contextual or language-model re-ranking stage. When the surface form alone underdetermines the answer, frequency is a weak proxy and accuracy falls. This is the same observation that motivates context-dependent over isolated-word correction in the classical literature [kukich1992techniques] and that drives neural re-ranking in modern grammatical error correction [omelianchuk2020gector; awasthi2019pie]; adding such a re-ranker is the clearest path to closing the drop/sub gap.

### 6.4 Qualitative Examples

The flagship behaviour is the recovery of *ka2ru*-corrupted Kazakh that superficially resembles Russian. The input «Бул маселе оте манызды болып тур», in which Kazakh-specific letters have been replaced by their Russian look-alikes, is first routed to the Kazakh path by the russified-lexicon signal (Section 4.5) and then restored to «Бұл мәселе өте маңызды болып тұр». Every edit here is a *ka2ru* inverse substitution applied within the {ә→а, ө→о, ұ→у, ү→у, қ→к, ғ→г, ң→н, і→и, һ→х} inverse map — for instance у→ұ (бул→бұл), а→ә (маселе→мәселе), о→ө (оте→өте), and н→ң (манызды→маңызды) — exactly the low-entropy regime in which the corrector excels.

Two further cases consistent with the error model illustrate the force-correct path and the drop/sub difficulty. In the force-correct case the detector flags a word that is itself a valid lexicon entry but wrong in context — for example «казак» is re-corrected to «қазақ» because the candidate's frequency dominates the flagged form; the corrector edits a lexicon-valid word only when a higher-frequency *ka2ru*-inverse candidate exists, guarding against gratuitous changes. By contrast, a deletion such as «мектп» (a dropped «е» from «мектеп») is recovered because only one frequent lexicon form lies within the edit radius, whereas an ambiguous deletion — where several short, frequent forms are equidistant from the corrupted token — is where the 62.0% drop rate originates: with no context model, the frequency tie-break cannot reliably choose the intended reconstruction. In all underdetermined cases the safe failure mode is to leave the token unchanged rather than emit a confidently wrong form. A systematic error analysis of over-corrections and false positives on real failure cases is left to future work (Section 8).

### 6.5 Efficiency and Deployment

The system is designed for CPU-only serving. A warm end-to-end request completes in roughly 9 ms (the corrector is reported to process on the order of a few milliseconds per word, though this per-word figure is not separately benchmarked); combined with single-threaded inference (`torch.set_num_threads(1)`) and a startup model warm-up, this yields a sustained throughput on the order of 200 requests per second on the test machine, which we treat as the CPU throughput ceiling for the current configuration. A single worker sustains roughly 150–193 req/s, and four workers approach the same ~200 req/s ceiling at a lower median latency (p50 ≈ 85 ms). We note that four workers yield little throughput gain over one, indicating a bottleneck (single-threaded torch, the Python GIL, or lexicon lookup) rather than linear scaling; we report this honestly and leave a remeasurement and profiling to future work. The deployed Docker container (image `turan-serve`, linux/arm64, torch 2.12.1+cpu) occupies approximately 373 MB of resident memory at runtime, and the fine-tuned detector checkpoint is 316.1 MiB (331,459,608 bytes) of safetensors weights. The system was validated in an accepted live pilot running in this container.

These characteristics make the system deployable on commodity hardware without a GPU; GPU execution and request batching would be required to scale substantially beyond ~200 req/s. The combination of a compact encoder, a frequency-indexed lexicon, and a deterministic rule/edit-distance corrector keeps the memory footprint and per-request latency low enough for interactive use, while the modular two-stage design leaves an explicit insertion point — contextual candidate re-ranking — for the accuracy improvements identified above.

## 7. Discussion

The evaluation supports the central design hypothesis *for the synthetic setting studied here*: for Kazakh spelling correction, error *detection* is essentially solved by a fine-tuned KazRoBERTa token classifier, while error *correction* is the remaining bottleneck. The detector reaches a test F1 of 97.3% (precision 97.1%, recall 97.4%), and the full pipeline corrects 80.5% of sentences end to end — within roughly three percentage points of the 83.5% oracle ceiling obtained under perfect detection. Because the gap between the trained pipeline and the oracle is small, further gains on this distribution will come predominantly from the corrector rather than from a stronger detector. As emphasised in Section 6.2, this conclusion is conditioned on the synthetic generator and its error rate; whether it holds on organic text is an open question that a human-annotated benchmark would answer.

The results also confirm that, within this dataset, the *ka2ru* phenomenon is both the dominant and the most tractable error class. It accounts for 30.5% of all injected edits (1,157,438 of the realized errors), the largest single share, and the corrector resolves it at 97.6% under oracle detection — second only to vowel-harmony violations at 97.9%. This pairing is not coincidental: *ka2ru* errors and harmony errors are structured, low-entropy substitutions over a small, closed set of look-alike or harmony-paired characters (e.g. қ↔к, ә↔а, ө↔о). The inverse-substitution candidate generator, filtered by lexicon membership, recovers the intended form almost deterministically because the confusion set is tiny and the correct candidate is overwhelmingly the one that is a valid Kazakh word. We restate the caveat from Section 6.3: because the same maps generate and invert these two classes, the headline per-type numbers are in-distribution and would be expected to drop on organic typing, where *ka2ru* and harmony errors co-occur with noisier corruptions. In practical terms, however, the most frequent real-world error in Kazakh typing — special letters replaced by visually similar standard-Russian Cyrillic because users lack or ignore the Kazakh layout — is also the one the system is designed around and handles best in this setting, which is the principal applied result of this work.

A tension surfaces between a *descriptive* corpus and a *normative* correction target. The lexicon is built from attested usage, so non-standard but commonly written forms are present and therefore treated as valid: for example, *салем* (frequency 6) and *рахмет* (frequency 221) coexist with their normative counterparts *сәлем* and *рақмет*. A descriptive lexicon faithfully reflects how Kazakh is actually written online, which is desirable for robust detection, but it limits a tool whose purpose is to enforce orthographic norms: a frequently attested non-normative spelling will not be flagged or rewritten. Resolving this tension requires an explicit normative layer rather than more data, since adding text only reinforces the descriptive distribution.

Finally, the approach is plausibly generalizable to other Cyrillic-script Turkic languages (e.g. Tatar, Bashkir, Kyrgyz), which share both the agglutinative morphology that motivates the morphological guard and the same socio-technical cause of look-alike substitution — language-specific Cyrillic letters absent from the standard Russian layout. The methodology — a reproducible cleaning stage, confusion-set synthetic error generation in the spirit of [grundkiewicz2019synthetic], a token-classification detector following the tag-not-rewrite paradigm [omelianchuk2020gector; awasthi2019pie], and an edit-distance corrector over a frequency-weighted lexicon [kukich1992techniques; damerau1964technique] — is language-agnostic in structure; only the confusion maps, lexicon, and morphological rules are language-specific. The "russified-lexicon" routing signal would transfer to any such language where letter-stripping makes the surface form resemble Russian.

### 7.1 Ethical Considerations

The system is intended for educational and assistive use — helping writers produce orthographically correct Kazakh — and it operates as a suggestion engine rather than an authority on usage. Two considerations follow. First, the model inherits a bias from its training corpus, which is drawn from a historical-register source (the `merged_dissertation.json` collection of historical Kazakh articles); the lexicon and learned error patterns reflect that register and may underperform on contemporary, colloquial, or domain-specific text. Users and downstream developers should be aware that "correctness" here is anchored to that corpus. Second, the pipeline processes only the text submitted for correction and does not collect, store, or require personal data; no user identifiers are part of the dataset or the serving path. Because the corrector's failure mode is to leave uncertain words unchanged rather than to overwrite them, the risk of silently corrupting a user's intended meaning is limited, though not eliminated for the harder drop and substitution cases.

## 8. Limitations

We separate the limitations into those intrinsic to the system and those reflecting the current state of the *evaluation*, since several reviewers will rightly weigh the latter heavily.

**Synthetic and partly circular evaluation.** Evaluation relies entirely on *synthetic*, programmatically injected errors (`build_kazakh_dataset.py`): the realized error mix and the per-type rates approximate, but do not equal, a human-typed error distribution, and there is no gold human-annotated test set. More specifically, the corrector's inverse-substitution generator uses exactly the *ka2ru* and harmony confusion maps that the generator uses to inject those errors. The per-type figures for those two classes (97.6% and 97.9%) and, to a lesser extent, the 80.5% end-to-end number are therefore measured partly against the system's own generative assumptions and are in-distribution by construction. The reported metrics should be read as in-distribution upper-leaning estimates, not as measurements against organic user errors. A few hundred manually annotated organic sentences — for which the accepted live-pilot traffic is an obvious source — are needed before the central claims can be regarded as established. This is the single most important limitation and the first priority for future work.

**Scope of the "detection is essentially solved" claim.** This claim rests solely on the ~3-point gap between the trained pipeline (80.5%) and the oracle (83.5%) on synthetic data at a fixed error rate (0.12, max 3 errors). It does not generalize beyond the synthetic generator and that error rate; on organic text with denser or differently distributed errors the detector's behaviour is unknown. Throughout the paper we have qualified the claim as holding "on this synthetic distribution," and it must be read that way.

**Missing competitive baselines.** The only learned-vs-heuristic comparison is KazRoBERTa versus a dictionary-lookup heuristic, which is a weak reference point. We do not compare against (a) a sequence-to-sequence / encoder-decoder GEC baseline (e.g. a fine-tuned mT5/ByT5 or character-level seq2seq); (b) the most directly comparable prior system, Baitenova et al. [baitenova2025hybrid]; (c) a multilingual encoder (e.g. XLM-R), which would test the monolingual-encoder choice for *this* task rather than rely on the general finding of [pires2019multilingual]; or (d) an off-the-shelf spellchecker (Hunspell/aspell with a Kazakh dictionary). The architectural choices are therefore motivated rather than empirically demonstrated. Adding at least some of these baselines is required future work.

**Absence of ablations.** No component is ablated. We provide no with/without numbers for the morphological guard, no measurement of how much the identity-pair fraction reduces over-correction, no comparison of ×3 versus ×1 augmentation, no ablation of the force-correct mode or of the two candidate generators, and no quantitative evaluation of the language router. For a methods paper each claimed design element should be backed by an ablation; supplying these is required future work.

**Unevaluated language-routing contribution.** The "russified-lexicon" routing signal is presented as a contribution but has no quantitative evaluation: there is no language-ID benchmark, no precision/recall on a Kazakh-vs-Russian routing task, and no comparison against fastText/CLD3/langid. Its threshold and weight constants (0.5 ramp, 0.6/0.7/0.1 weights, length ≥ 3, frequency ≥ 3) are hand-set and untuned. We have accordingly framed it in Section 4.5 as an engineering mechanism and identify a proper evaluation as future work; until then it should be read as an engineering detail rather than a validated contribution.

**Corrector weaknesses.** The corrector is weakest precisely where local, character-level evidence is insufficient: drop (deletion) at 62% and substitution (sub) at 70% under oracle detection. These classes often admit several lexically valid candidates, and the system ranks candidates by edit distance with a frequency tie-break, without any contextual or language-model re-ranking. The morphological guard, moreover, covers nominal morphology only; valid verbal forms are not protected and could in principle be mangled by the edit-distance fallback.

**Lexicon and corpus constraints.** The corpus is descriptive rather than normative, so attested non-standard forms (e.g. *салем*, *рахмет*) are treated as valid and are not auto-corrected. The corrector is also bounded by the lexicon (339,717 surface forms; 174,100 valid at frequency ≥ 2): out-of-vocabulary items such as rare proper names and neologisms are not generated as candidates. The associated failure mode is conservative — such words are left unchanged rather than mangled — but genuine errors in OOV contexts are missed.

**Short fragments and the Russian path.** The detector and the language router are weaker on very short fragments (one to two tokens), since the model was trained on full sentences and short inputs provide little context. The Russian correction path is a deliberate no-op: there is no Russian model, so misrouted valid Russian text could be left unhelpfully unchanged, and Russian correction is explicitly out of scope.

**Serving throughput.** With `torch.set_num_threads(1)` and synchronous endpoints dispatched to a threadpool, warm latency is roughly 9 ms per request, and throughput is approximately 150–193 requests per second on a single worker and about 200 requests per second with four workers — an effective CPU ceiling near 200 requests per second. The near-flat scaling from one to four workers points to a bottleneck (single-thread torch, the GIL, or lexicon lookup) that we have not profiled; scaling beyond the ceiling would require GPU execution and request batching.

**Statistical rigor and metric definitions.** All numbers come from a single training run (one seed) on a single fixed 8,000-sentence subset, with no confidence intervals or significance tests; multiple seeds and bootstrap confidence intervals are needed before the gaps can be assessed for robustness. The end-to-end denominator includes identity (clean) pairs, which are trivially "fully corrected" and inflate the absolute percentages; a clean-pair-excluded variant should also be reported. We have defined the end-to-end metric (exact sentence match after correction) and precision-of-changes (correctly-applied changes / total applied changes) explicitly, but we use a homegrown "fully corrected" metric rather than standard GEC metrics (M2/ERRANT, GLEU); adopting a standard metric would improve comparability.

**Reproducibility gaps.** A number of secondary figures could not be re-derived from a verifiable artefact in this pass and are marked [TODO: verify] in the tables and text: detector and heuristic false-positive counts, cold-start latency, single-worker p50/p99 point estimates, the ERROR-token training fraction, corrector per-word speed, the corpus article count and license, the encoder layer/head/hidden counts, and the exact Python/PyTorch version strings. None of these are load-bearing for the headline results, and we resolve to confirm or remove each before camera-ready.

## 9. Conclusion and Future Work

We presented *Turan Text Corrector*, a two-stage Kazakh spelling-correction system that pairs a KazRoBERTa token-classification detector (test F1 97.3%) with a lexicon and edit-distance corrector guarded by a morphological analyzer, reaching 80.5% sentence-level correction end to end — near an 83.5% oracle ceiling on a synthetic test set — and validated in an accepted live pilot. The work centers on the under-studied *ka2ru* look-alike error class, the dominant error type in this dataset (30.5% of injected edits) and the one the system corrects most reliably (97.6%), and contributes a lightweight "russified-lexicon" routing signal that recovers special-letter-stripped Kazakh that would otherwise be misread as Russian. We are deliberate that the empirical results are synthetic and in-distribution; the durable contributions we claim are the problem framing, the data-construction methodology, and a deployable engineered system.

Future work follows directly from the limitations and is, in our view, necessary to convert the present results into established findings.

1. **A human-annotated benchmark.** Collect and annotate a few hundred organically typed Kazakh sentences (e.g. from pilot traffic) to measure performance against the real error distribution and to test the synthetic-vs-organic similarity assumption. This is the first priority.
2. **Competitive baselines.** Compare against a seq2seq GEC model (mT5/ByT5 or character-level seq2seq), a multilingual encoder (XLM-R), an off-the-shelf spellchecker, and the hybrid system of Baitenova et al. [baitenova2025hybrid] on a shared task, and report standard GEC metrics (M2/ERRANT, GLEU) alongside the fully-corrected metric.
3. **Component ablations and statistical rigor.** Ablate the morphological guard, force-correct mode, identity-pair fraction, ×3 augmentation, and the two candidate generators; train multiple seeds and report bootstrap confidence intervals and significance tests; report the end-to-end metric with clean pairs excluded.
4. **A quantitative language-router evaluation.** Build a Kazakh-vs-Russian routing benchmark (including *ka2ru*-corrupted Kazakh), report precision/recall/F1, compare against fastText/CLD3/langid, and tune the router constants.
5. **A contextual re-ranker and a normative layer.** Integrate a Kazakh language model to score candidates in context — the clearest route to closing the drop (62%) and substitution (70%) gaps — and add an explicit normative lexicon plus a dedicated short-word module to address the descriptive-versus-normative tension and weak short-input handling. Extending verbal-morphology coverage in the guard, and extending the approach to Latin-script Kazakh and other Cyrillic-script Turkic languages, are natural follow-ups.
