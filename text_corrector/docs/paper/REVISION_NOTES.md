## 3. Reviewer-2 assessment — recommendation: **major revision**

> The paper addresses a genuinely interesting and practically relevant problem -- the 'ka2ru' Cyrillic look-alike substitution in Kazakh and its dual orthographic and language-routing consequences -- and the framing of this specific phenomenon at corpus scale is the work's most novel and defensible contribution. The two-stage detect-then-correct architecture is sensible, well-engineered, and clearly written, and the system is demonstrably deployable (CPU-only, compact, piloted). The data construction is careful (exact-edit metadata, identity pairs, leakage-free splits) and the limitations section is honest about synthetic data. However, the empirical foundation is too weak for the strength of the claims. The evaluation is entirely synthetic and, more seriously, partly circular: the corrector inverts the very confusion maps used to inject the errors, so headline numbers (97.6% ka2ru, 80.5% end-to-end, 'detection essentially solved') largely reflect the generator's assumptions rather than real-world performance. There are no competitive baselines (no seq2seq, no multilingual encoder, no comparison to the closest prior system it cites), no ablations of any of the four claimed contributions, and the language-routing contribution -- prominent in the abstract -- has zero quantitative evaluation. Reproducibility is compromised by multiple [TODO: verify] fields, including false-positive counts that are stated as hard numbers in the prose but marked unverifiable in the tables. The clarity, novelty of the problem framing, and engineering are real strengths, but as submitted the contributions are not justified by the evidence presented. Substantial additional experiments (a small gold test set, real baselines, ablations, language-router evaluation, statistical rigor) are required.

### Major issues (address before submission)

- Evaluation is entirely synthetic with no human-annotated test set, and the corruption is generated by the SAME procedure (build_kazakh_dataset.py, fixed confusion maps) that the corrector explicitly inverts. The corrector's inverse-substitution generator uses exactly the ka2ru/harmony maps used to inject errors, so the 97.6%/97.9% per-type numbers and the 80.5% end-to-end figure are measuring the system against its own generative assumptions. This is a near-circular evaluation: the test set is in-distribution by construction. The paper acknowledges synthetic-only data as a limitation but does not acknowledge this tighter circularity, which inflates the headline results. At minimum a few hundred manually annotated organic sentences (the live-pilot traffic is an obvious source) are needed before the central claims can be supported.
- Overclaiming in the central thesis: 'detection is essentially solved.' This claim is derived solely from the 3-point gap between trained pipeline (80.5%) and oracle (83.5%) on synthetic data. The conclusion does not generalize beyond the synthetic generator and the specific error rate (0.12, max 3 errors). On organic text with denser or different errors the detector behavior is unknown. The abstract, introduction, results, and discussion all repeat 'detection is essentially solved' as if it were a general finding; it must be heavily qualified as 'on this synthetic distribution.'
- Missing baselines required for a Scopus venue. The only learned-vs-heuristic comparison is KazRoBERTa vs a dictionary-lookup heuristic, which is a strawman. There is no comparison against: (a) a seq2seq / encoder-decoder GEC baseline (e.g., a fine-tuned mT5/ByT5 or character-level seq2seq), which the paper repeatedly dismisses on a priori grounds but never empirically tests; (b) the most directly comparable prior system, Baitenova et al. 2025 (hybrid FST+CRF+KazRoBERTa/mBERT), which is cited as the closest work but never compared against, even on a shared or reconstructed task; (c) a multilingual encoder baseline (e.g., XLM-R) to justify the monolingual-encoder claim for THIS task rather than by citing pires2019multilingual in general; (d) an off-the-shelf spellchecker (Hunspell/aspell with a Kazakh dictionary). Without at least some of these, the contribution cannot be situated and the architectural choices are asserted, not demonstrated.
- Critical missing ablations. The system has multiple components (morphological guard, force-correct mode, language router, identity-pair fraction, x3 augmentation, two candidate generators) and not a single one is ablated. There is no evidence that the morphological guard helps (no with/without numbers), no measurement of how much the identity-pair fraction actually reduces over-correction, no ablation of x3 augmentation vs x1, no ablation of force-correct mode, and no quantitative evaluation of the language router at all (only one cherry-picked qualitative example). For a methods paper, every claimed design contribution must be backed by an ablation; otherwise the contributions are not justified by evidence.
- The 'russified-lexicon' language-routing signal is presented as a principal contribution (contribution 3, prominent in abstract/intro) but has ZERO quantitative evaluation. There is no language-ID benchmark, no precision/recall on a Kazakh-vs-Russian routing task, no comparison against fastText/CLD3/langid, and the threshold/weight constants (0.5 ramp, 0.6/0.7/0.1 weights, length>=3, freq>=3) are unmotivated and untuned/unvalidated. A contribution claimed in the abstract with no supporting experiment is not acceptable; this must either be evaluated properly or demoted from a contribution to an engineering detail.
- Reproducibility is undermined by numerous [TODO: verify] markers in load-bearing places: the detector's false-positive counts (97.3% F1 detector FP count is [TODO: verify] in Table 2, yet the results text confidently states 327 FPs and the heuristic's 1,818 FPs and the '5.6x' claim -- these numbers appear in prose but are marked unverifiable in the table, an internal contradiction), heuristic baseline precision/recall entirely [TODO: verify], corpus license [TODO: verify], article count [TODO: verify], ERROR-token fraction [TODO: verify], corrector speed [TODO: verify]. A paper cannot report a quantitative claim (327 vs 1,818 FPs, 5.6x) in the body while marking the same quantity as unverifiable in the table. Either the numbers are real and must appear in the table, or they must be removed from the prose.
- No statistical rigor whatsoever: no confidence intervals, no significance tests, single training run (one seed), single evaluation subset. The headline gaps (e.g., 80.5 vs 83.5, or per-type 70% vs 62%) are reported to one decimal with no variance. With a single seed and no CIs, none of the comparisons can be assessed for robustness. At least multiple seeds for the detector and bootstrap CIs on the sentence-level metric are expected.

### Minor issues

- Inconsistent source-corpus size: stated as 568,374,253 bytes '(approximately 568 MB)' in Section 3 but '(approx 542 MiB)' in Table 1. 568,374,253 bytes is indeed ~542 MiB or ~568 MB; pick MB or MiB consistently and reconcile the two sections.
- Train-pair provenance is inconsistent: Section 3 says training pairs come 'from 872,798 source sentences under x3 augmentation' but also states the cleaned corpus has 922,798 unique sentences. The 50,000 difference (922,798 - 872,798 = 50,000 = val+test sentences) is implied but never stated; make explicit that 50k sentences were held out for val/test before x3 augmentation. Also 872,798 x 3 = 2,618,394, not 2,565,771; the discrepancy (~52k) needs explanation (identity pairs? dedup?).
- Evaluation subset inconsistency: the test split is 25,000 pairs, but detection and end-to-end metrics are reported on an '8,000-sentence subset.' Why not the full 25,000? The subsetting is unexplained and could invite cherry-picking concerns; report on the full test set or justify the subset and confirm it was fixed in advance.
- The detector is described as '6 layers' (Methods) which is unusually small for a RoBERTa encoder; confirm this is correct for kaz-roberta-conversational and not a typo, since it affects the 316 MiB checkpoint and capacity claims.
- PyTorch version is implausible/anachronistic: 'torch 2.12.1+cpu' / 'PyTorch 2.12' and 'Python 3.13'. As of the stated dataset/work era these versions are questionable (torch 2.12 did not exist at typical writing time). Verify exact versions; a wrong version harms reproducibility credibility.
- The qualitative ka2ru example contains an apparent error: the text lists edits including 'е->ә' and 'ы->ы' (a no-op) and 'ң restored', which is garbled. The mapping for «оте»->«өте» is о->ө, not е->ә; listing 'ы->ы' as an edit is meaningless. This worked example should be corrected and made internally consistent.
- Precision-of-changes (91.6%) and 'fixed 88%' under oracle are reported, but the denominator/definition of 'precision-of-changes' is never formally defined. Define it (changes that match gold / total changes applied).
- The morphological guard is described only for nominal morphology (STEM+NUMBER+POSS+CASE); Kazakh verbal morphology is far richer. State explicitly that verbal forms are not guarded and quantify the resulting risk, since the edit-distance fallback could mangle valid verb forms.
- Throughput figures are internally loose: '150-193 req/s' single worker and '~200 req/s' four workers means 4x workers gives almost no gain, suggesting a bottleneck elsewhere (GIL, single-thread torch, lexicon lookup). This near-zero scaling is not discussed and weakens the deployment story; explain or remeasure.
- Related work omits standard GEC evaluation metrics (M2/ERRANT, GLEU). The paper uses only a homegrown 'fully corrected sentence' metric; adding a standard GEC metric would aid comparability with the broader GEC literature it positions itself within.
- Citation/provenance mismatch: [makhambetov2013kazcorpus] is cited as the lexicon/corpus source in Methods and Related Work, but Section 3 clearly states the data come from the ehistory 'merged_dissertation.json' historical corpus, not the Kazakh Language Corpus. This misrepresents the actual data source and must be fixed.

### Missing elements

- A human-annotated (gold) test set of organically typed Kazakh errors, or at least a small validation of synthetic-vs-organic error distribution similarity. Currently the entire empirical claim rests on synthetic data.
- Ablation studies for every claimed component: morphological guard, force-correct mode, identity-pair fraction, x3 augmentation, the two candidate generators, and the language router.
- Quantitative evaluation of the language-routing contribution (precision/recall/F1 on a Kazakh-vs-Russian routing benchmark, vs fastText/CLD3/langid baselines).
- Comparative baselines: a seq2seq GEC model, a multilingual encoder (XLM-R), and a direct comparison to Baitenova et al. 2025; ideally an off-the-shelf spellchecker.
- Statistical reporting: multiple seeds, confidence intervals (bootstrap on sentence-level metric), and significance tests for the key comparisons.
- Resolution of all [TODO: verify] fields, especially the detector and heuristic false-positive counts that are quoted as concrete numbers (327, 1,818, 5.6x) in the results prose.
- Figures: an architecture diagram and the error-type distribution figure are referenced ('Figure (architecture)', 'Figure X') but no figures are present in the provided files; these must be supplied.
- An error analysis on actual failure cases, especially over-corrections / false positives that change correct words, with examples, not just aggregate per-type accuracy.
- Detector error breakdown by error type (does it miss drops more than ka2ru?), which would substantiate the per-class 'detection essentially solved' claim.
- Precise formal definitions of the end-to-end and precision-of-changes metrics, and clarity on whether identity (clean) pairs are included in the 80.5%/83.5% denominators (their inclusion would inflate 'fully corrected,' since clean pairs are trivially 'corrected').
- Corpus licensing resolution and a concrete data/code availability statement (release is described as conditional with a [TODO: verify] on license).
