## DISCUSSION

The evaluation supports the central design hypothesis: for Kazakh spelling correction, error *detection* is essentially solved by a fine-tuned KazRoBERTa token classifier, while error *correction* is the remaining bottleneck. The detector reaches a test F1 of 97.3% (precision 97.1%, recall 97.4%), and the full pipeline corrects 80.5% of sentences end to end — within roughly three percentage points of the 83.5% oracle ceiling obtained under perfect detection. Because the gap between the trained pipeline and the oracle is small, further gains will come predominantly from the corrector rather than from a stronger detector.

The results also confirm that the *ka2ru* phenomenon is both the dominant and the most tractable error class. It accounts for 30.5% of all injected edits (1,157,438 of the realized errors), the largest single share, and the corrector resolves it at 97.6% under oracle detection — second only to vowel-harmony violations at 97.9%. This pairing is not coincidental: *ka2ru* errors and harmony errors are structured, low-entropy substitutions over a small, closed set of look-alike or harmony-paired characters (e.g. $\text{қ}\leftrightarrow\text{к}$, $\text{ә}\leftrightarrow\text{а}$, $\text{ө}\leftrightarrow\text{о}$). The inverse-substitution candidate generator, filtered by lexicon membership, recovers the intended form almost deterministically because the confusion set is tiny and the correct candidate is overwhelmingly the one that is a valid Kazakh word. In practical terms, the most frequent real-world error in Kazakh typing — special letters replaced by visually similar standard-Russian Cyrillic because users lack or ignore the Kazakh layout — is also the one the system handles best, which is the principal applied result of this work.

A tension surfaces between a *descriptive* corpus and a *normative* correction target. The lexicon is built from attested usage, so non-standard but commonly written forms are present and therefore treated as valid: for example, *салем* (frequency 6) and *рахмет* (frequency 221) coexist with their normative counterparts *сәлем* and *рақмет*. A descriptive lexicon faithfully reflects how Kazakh is actually written online, which is desirable for robust detection, but it limits a tool whose purpose is to enforce orthographic norms: a frequently attested non-normative spelling will not be flagged or rewritten. Resolving this tension requires an explicit normative layer rather than more data, since adding text only reinforces the descriptive distribution.

Finally, the approach is plausibly generalizable to other Cyrillic-script Turkic languages (e.g. Tatar, Bashkir, Kyrgyz), which share both the agglutinative morphology that motivates the morphological guard and the same socio-technical cause of look-alike substitution — language-specific Cyrillic letters absent from the standard Russian layout. The methodology — a reproducible cleaning stage, confusion-set synthetic error generation in the spirit of [grundkiewicz2019synthetic], a token-classification detector following the tag-not-rewrite paradigm [omelianchuk2020gector; awasthi2019pie], and an edit-distance corrector over a frequency-weighted lexicon [kukich1992techniques; damerau1964technique] — is language-agnostic in structure; only the confusion maps, lexicon, and morphological rules are language-specific. The "russified-lexicon" routing signal would transfer to any such language where letter-stripping makes the surface form resemble Russian.

## LIMITATIONS

Several limitations constrain the scope of these claims. First, evaluation relies on *synthetic*, programmatically injected errors (build_kazakh_dataset.py): the realized error mix and the per-type rates approximate, but do not equal, a human-typed error distribution, and there is no gold human-annotated test set. The reported metrics should therefore be read as in-distribution with respect to the synthetic generator, not as a measurement against organic user errors.

Second, the corrector is weakest precisely where local, character-level evidence is insufficient: drop (deletion) at 62% and substitution (sub) at 70% under oracle detection. These classes often admit several lexically valid candidates, and the system ranks candidates by edit distance with a frequency tie-break, without any contextual or language-model re-ranking; the sentence context that would disambiguate them is not used at correction time.

Third, the corpus is descriptive rather than normative, so attested non-standard forms (e.g. *салем*, *рахмет*) are treated as valid and are not auto-corrected. The corrector is also bounded by the lexicon (339,717 surface forms; 174,100 valid at frequency $\geq 2$): out-of-vocabulary items such as rare proper names and neologisms are not generated as candidates. The associated failure mode is conservative — such words are left unchanged rather than mangled — but genuine errors in OOV contexts are missed.

Fourth, the detector and the language router are weaker on very short fragments (one to two tokens), since the model was trained on full sentences and short inputs provide little context; short-input routing is correspondingly harder. Relatedly, the Russian correction path is a deliberate no-op: there is no Russian model, so misrouted valid Russian text could in principle be left unhelpfully unchanged, and Russian correction is explicitly out of scope.

Fifth, serving throughput is bounded on CPU. With torch.set_num_threads(1) and synchronous endpoints dispatched to a threadpool, warm latency is roughly 9 ms per request, and throughput is approximately 150–193 requests per second on a single worker and about 200 requests per second with four workers — an effective CPU ceiling near 200 requests per second on the test hardware. Scaling beyond this would require GPU execution and request batching.

## ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

The system is intended for educational and assistive use — helping writers produce orthographically correct Kazakh — and it operates as a suggestion engine rather than an authority on usage. Two considerations follow. First, the model inherits a bias from its training corpus, which is drawn from a historical-register source (the merged_dissertation.json collection of historical Kazakh articles); the lexicon and learned error patterns reflect that register and may underperform on contemporary, colloquial, or domain-specific text. Users and downstream developers should be aware that "correctness" here is anchored to that corpus. Second, the pipeline processes only the text submitted for correction and does not collect, store, or require personal data; no user identifiers are part of the dataset or the serving path. Because the corrector's failure mode is to leave uncertain words unchanged rather than to overwrite them, the risk of silently corrupting a user's intended meaning is limited, though not eliminated for the harder drop and substitution cases.

## REPRODUCIBILITY AND DATA AVAILABILITY

The pipeline is built to be reproducible. Corpus cleaning (kk_text.py) and synthetic error generation (build_kazakh_dataset.py) are deterministic given a fixed random seed (seed = 42), so the parallel dataset — 2,615,771 total pairs (train 2,565,771 from 872,798 sentences with $\times 3$ variant augmentation; validation 25,000; test 25,000), of which 10.86% are clean identity pairs — can be regenerated from the source corpus and the published scripts. The detector training configuration is fully specified (KazRoBERTa backbone kz-transformers/kaz-roberta-conversational [kztransformers2023kazroberta; sagyndyk2025kazroberta]; token classification with labels KEEP/ERROR; learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$; batch size 64; two epochs; maximum length 128; mixed-precision fp16; AdamW optimizer with a linear schedule and no warmup at default settings), and the fine-tuned checkpoint is a single safetensors file of 331,459,608 bytes (316.1 MiB). Lexicon construction (build_lexicon.py) and the corrector, language-routing, and morphology modules are likewise scriptable. The system is deployable CPU-only via a Docker image (turan-serve, linux/arm64, torch 2.12.1+cpu, 2.84 GB; running container approximately 373 MB RAM). What can be released without further dependencies includes the cleaning and generation scripts, the lexicon-building script, the model configuration and hyperparameters, and the evaluation scripts; release of the derived dataset and the source corpus is subject to the licensing terms of the underlying historical-article collection [TODO: verify corpus license].

## CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

We presented Turan Text Corrector, a two-stage Kazakh spelling-correction system that pairs a KazRoBERTa token-classification detector (test F1 97.3%) with a lexicon and edit-distance corrector guarded by a morphological analyzer, reaching 80.5% sentence-level correction end to end — near the 83.5% oracle ceiling — and validated in an accepted live pilot. The work centers on the under-studied *ka2ru* look-alike error class, the dominant error type in practice (30.5% of injected edits) and the one the system corrects most reliably (97.6%), and contributes a lightweight "russified-lexicon" routing signal that recovers special-letter-stripped Kazakh that would otherwise be misread as Russian.

Future work follows directly from the limitations. First, an explicit normative lexicon and a dedicated short-word module would address both the descriptive-versus-normative tension and the weaker handling of one-to-two-token inputs. Second, contextual or language-model re-ranking of candidates is the most promising route to improving the drop (62%) and substitution (70%) classes, where local edit distance alone is insufficient; integrating a Kazakh language model [liu2019roberta; sagyndyk2025kazroberta] to score candidates in context would let the corrector use the sentence evidence it currently ignores. Third, a human-annotated benchmark of organically typed Kazakh errors is needed to measure performance against the real error distribution rather than the synthetic one. Finally, extending the approach to Latin-script Kazakh — and, by the same methodology, to other Cyrillic-script Turkic languages — would broaden the system's applicability as Kazakh orthography continues to evolve.
