# Introduction

Kazakh is a Turkic language written in a Cyrillic script and is, by the standards of contemporary natural language processing (NLP), a low-resource language: large annotated corpora, gold-standard error-correction datasets, and mature downstream tooling remain comparatively scarce. It is also strongly agglutinative, forming words by concatenating long sequences of suffixes that encode number, possession, case, and other categories onto a stem under vowel harmony. The combination of rich productive morphology and limited supervision makes Kazakh text processing difficult: the effective vocabulary is enormous, surface forms proliferate, and fixed lexicons cover only a fraction of the inflected forms that occur in practice. Subword tokenization mitigates the vocabulary explosion [sennrich2016bpe], and Transformer encoders pretrained with masked language modeling have become the standard backbone for sequence-labeling tasks in such settings [vaswani2017attention; devlin2019bert; liu2019roberta]. Dedicated monolingual models tend to outperform multilingual ones on individual languages, motivating Kazakh-specific encoders rather than reliance on multilingual checkpoints [pires2019multilingual]. KazRoBERTa, a RoBERTa-style encoder pretrained on Kazakh, is one such model and is now publicly available [kztransformers2023kazroberta; sagyndyk2025kazroberta].

A specific and widespread source of textual noise in Kazakh writing motivates this work. The Kazakh Cyrillic alphabet extends the Russian one with nine letters that have no Russian counterpart: $\text{қ}$, $\text{ә}$, $\text{ң}$, $\text{ұ}$, $\text{ү}$, $\text{ғ}$, $\text{і}$, $\text{ө}$, and $\text{һ}$. Because users frequently lack a Kazakh keyboard layout, or ignore it for convenience, they substitute the visually similar standard-Russian Cyrillic letters: $\text{қ}{\to}\text{к}$, $\text{ә}{\to}\text{а}$, $\text{ң}{\to}\text{н}$, $\text{ұ}{\to}\text{у}$, $\text{ү}{\to}\text{у}$, $\text{ғ}{\to}\text{г}$, $\text{і}{\to}\text{и}$, $\text{ө}{\to}\text{о}$, and $\text{һ}{\to}\text{х}$. We refer to this error class as *ka2ru*. The result is still Cyrillic text, but it is no longer correct Kazakh: special letters are systematically lost, and the surface form frequently resembles broken Russian. This phenomenon harms downstream NLP in two distinct ways. First, the words themselves are corrupted, degrading any task that depends on correct orthography, from lexical lookup to search and indexing. Second, and more insidiously, it breaks language identification: because the discriminative Kazakh letters are exactly the ones removed, a ka2ru-corrupted Kazakh sentence can be misclassified as Russian, causing it to be routed to the wrong processing path entirely. The error is therefore not merely cosmetic; it propagates into and corrupts pipeline-level decisions.

Despite its prevalence, this exact phenomenon has received little focused attention at scale. Prior Kazakh language technology has emphasized foundational resources — corpora [makhambetov2013kazcorpus], named-entity datasets [yeshpanov2022kaznerd], rule-based and finite-state morphological analyzers [kessikbayeva2014kazakh; washington2014kypchak], machine translation [salimzyanov2013kazakhtatar], and treebanks [tyers2015kazakhud] — while recent hybrid systems combine finite-state methods, conditional random fields, and Transformer encoders for Kazakh text correction [baitenova2025hybrid]. General spelling correction has a long history of nonword detection and isolated correction [kukich1992techniques], with edit-distance models underpinning candidate ranking [levenshtein1966binary; damerau1964technique], and modern grammatical error correction increasingly frames the task as edit tagging over a pretrained encoder rather than full rewriting [awasthi2019pie; omelianchuk2020gector], often trained on synthetically generated error pairs [grundkiewicz2019synthetic]. What is largely missing is a system that targets the ka2ru look-alike substitution specifically, at corpus scale, and that addresses both its orthographic and its language-routing consequences.

This paper presents *Turan Text Corrector*, an automatic spelling- and typo-correction system for Kazakh built around the ka2ru phenomenon. We construct a reproducible cleaning pipeline and a synthetic-error generator over a large historical-text corpus, producing a parallel dataset whose training split contains $2{,}565{,}771$ corrupted–correct pairs (and $2{,}615{,}771$ pairs in total across train, validation, and test); the dominant injected error class is ka2ru, accounting for $30.5\%$ of all edits. The system itself is a two-stage pipeline: a KazRoBERTa token-classification detector that labels each token KEEP or ERROR, feeding a corrector that combines inverse look-alike substitutions, a frequency-weighted lexicon, restricted Damerau–Levenshtein (Optimal String Alignment) candidate search, and morphological guarding of valid inflected forms. To address misrouting, we introduce a lightweight "russified-lexicon" language-routing signal that maps the Kazakh lexicon through the ka2ru substitution and uses length-aware, nonlinear, Russian-stopword-excluded coverage to recover special-letter-stripped Kazakh that would otherwise be read as Russian. On the held-out test set, the detector attains an $F_1$ of $97.3\%$ (precision $97.1\%$, recall $97.4\%$), and the full pipeline corrects $80.5\%$ of sentences end to end — within roughly three points of the $83.5\%$ oracle ceiling obtained with perfect detection — substantially exceeding a heuristic baseline ($64.6\%$ end to end). The system runs on CPU at a warm latency of about $9$ ms per request and was validated in an accepted live pilot. The principal contributions of this work are:

- **A reproducible cleaning and synthetic-error-generation methodology** for Kazakh spelling correction, yielding a large parallel dataset (training split of $2{,}565{,}771$ pairs; $2{,}615{,}771$ pairs in total) centered on the under-studied ka2ru look-alike phenomenon, with explicit edit metadata and an identity-pair fraction of $10.86\%$ to suppress over-correction.
- **A two-stage detection-then-correction pipeline** pairing a KazRoBERTa token-classification error detector (test $F_1 = 97.3\%$) with a lexicon- and edit-distance-based corrector that uses morphological guarding, reaching $80.5\%$ end-to-end sentence-level correction — within about three points of the $83.5\%$ oracle ceiling — and indicating that detection is essentially solved while the corrector is the remaining bottleneck.
- **A lightweight "russified-lexicon" language-routing signal** that maps the Kazakh lexicon through the ka2ru substitution and applies nonlinear, length-aware, Russian-stopword-excluded scoring, directly addressing the language-identification failure mode that the ka2ru error class creates.
- **A deployable, CPU-only system** with a throughput ceiling of approximately $200$ requests per second, a running-container footprint of about $373$ MB of RAM, and a $2.84$ GB linux/arm64 image (torch $2.12.1$+cpu), validated in an accepted live pilot.
