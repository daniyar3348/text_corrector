## Related Work

### Transformer Encoders and RoBERTa

The Transformer architecture [vaswani2017attention] introduced fully attention-based sequence modelling and is the foundation of modern pretrained encoders. BERT [devlin2019bert] established the masked-language-modelling (MLM) paradigm, in which a bidirectional encoder is pretrained on large unlabelled corpora and subsequently fine-tuned for downstream tasks, including per-token labelling. RoBERTa [liu2019roberta] refined this recipe through longer training, larger batches, dynamic masking, and the removal of the next-sentence-prediction objective, yielding consistently stronger representations. A practical prerequisite for applying these models to morphologically rich languages is subword tokenization; byte-pair encoding [sennrich2016bpe] segments words into reusable units and mitigates the out-of-vocabulary problem that an agglutinative language such as Kazakh would otherwise present. Our detector builds directly on this lineage: it fine-tunes KazRoBERTa [kztransformers2023kazroberta; sagyndyk2025kazroberta], a RoBERTa-style encoder pretrained with MLM on Kazakh text using a byte-level BPE vocabulary, as a token-classification model that emits a binary KEEP/ERROR label per token.

### Kazakh, Turkic, and Low-Resource NLP and Morphology

Kazakh is an agglutinative Turkic language whose inflection is governed by vowel harmony (сингармонизм), making morphological well-formedness a useful signal for distinguishing genuine words from corrupted ones. Foundational language resources include the Kazakh Language Corpus [makhambetov2013kazcorpus], which provides large-scale clean text suitable as a source for synthetic data, and the Kazakh Universal Dependencies treebank [tyers2015kazakhud], which adds syntactic annotation. Token-level supervised tasks for Kazakh have been formalized through datasets such as KazNERD [yeshpanov2022kaznerd], establishing sequence-labelling as a viable setup for the language and motivating our framing of error detection as token classification. Rule-based morphological processing for Kazakh and related Kypchak languages has a substantial open-source tradition: two-level and finite-state analyzers [kessikbayeva2014kazakh; washington2014kypchak] segment a surface form into a stem and ordered NUMBER/POSS/CASE suffixes and verify harmony, and broader rule-based pipelines have been developed for Kazakh–Tatar machine translation [salimzyanov2013kazakhtatar]. Because dedicated monolingual encoders tend to outperform multilingual models on cross-lingual transfer for individual languages [pires2019multilingual], we adopt a Kazakh-specific encoder rather than a multilingual baseline. Our system reuses the morphological-analysis idea as a *guard*: a lightweight analyzer that segments stem and suffixes and validates vowel harmony is used to leave well-formed inflected forms untouched, rather than as a standalone correction engine.

### Grammatical Error Detection, Correction, and Spelling Correction

Automatic correction of written text has been studied since early work on isolated-word and context-dependent spelling correction; Kukich [kukich1992techniques] surveys the classical decomposition into non-word detection and candidate ranking that still structures the field. More recent grammatical error correction (GEC) has converged on neural sequence-labelling rather than full sequence-to-sequence rewriting. Parallel Iterative Edit models [awasthi2019pie] cast correction as predicting local edit tags over a BERT encoder, and GECToR [omelianchuk2020gector] demonstrated that a Transformer tagger ("tag, not rewrite") is both fast and accurate. Our pipeline shares this token-classification philosophy but deliberately separates the two sub-problems: a learned detector identifies *where* errors occur, while a deterministic corrector decides *how* to fix them. The most directly comparable Kazakh-specific work is the hybrid architecture of Baitenova et al. [baitenova2025hybrid], which combines finite-state transducers, conditional random fields, and KazRoBERTa/mBERT for Kazakh text correction. We differ in scope and emphasis: we target spelling and typing errors centred on a specific, under-studied phenomenon — the "ka2ru" class, in which Kazakh-specific Cyrillic letters (қ ә ң ұ ү ғ і ө һ) are typed as visually similar standard-Russian Cyrillic letters because the Kazakh keyboard layout is unavailable or ignored — and we pair the detector with a transparent lexicon- and edit-distance-based corrector with morphological guarding. On a synthetic test set, our detector reaches an $F_1$ of 97.3% and the full pipeline corrects 80.5% of sentences completely, within roughly three percentage points of the 83.5% oracle ceiling obtained under perfect detection.

### Synthetic Error Generation and Data Augmentation for GEC

A persistent obstacle for GEC in any language is the scarcity of annotated parallel data, which is especially acute for Kazakh. A standard remedy is to inject artificial errors into clean text and train on the resulting synthetic pairs. Grundkiewicz et al. [grundkiewicz2019synthetic] showed that confusion-set-based synthetic corruption, followed by training on the generated pairs, substantially improves neural GEC. We adapt this strategy to Kazakh spelling correction: clean sentences derived from a cleaned historical-article corpus are corrupted by six error operations — ka2ru look-alike substitution, vowel-harmony violation, character swap, deletion, duplication, and substitution — to form a parallel dataset. The realized distribution is dominated by the ka2ru class (30.5% of injected edits), followed by harmony (21.3%) and swap (17.9%). The training split comprises 2,565,771 corrupted/correct pairs (the full dataset, including validation and test, totals 2,615,771 pairs), of which approximately 11% are identity (clean-to-clean) pairs included to suppress over-correction. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale synthetic dataset for Kazakh spelling correction organized around the ka2ru look-alike phenomenon. A known limitation, shared with synthetic-data approaches generally, is that programmatically injected errors approximate but do not equal the distribution of human-typed errors; no gold human-annotated Kazakh test set is yet available.

### Edit-Distance Methods

Candidate ranking in spelling correction has long relied on string-edit metrics. Levenshtein distance [levenshtein1966binary] counts the minimal insertions, deletions, and substitutions between two strings, while Damerau [damerau1964technique] additionally modelled adjacent-character transposition, capturing a large fraction of single-character human typing errors. Our corrector's fallback candidate search uses Optimal String Alignment distance — a restricted form of Damerau–Levenshtein with transposition cost 1 — over a frequency-weighted lexicon of 339,717 surface forms (174,100 with frequency $\geq 2$), with a frequency tie-break. Rather than relying on edit distance alone, we first generate candidates by inverting the language-specific ka2ru and harmony substitutions and filtering by lexicon membership, falling back to edit-distance search only when needed. This combination of classical edit-distance ranking with language-specific confusion sets and morphological guarding is what differentiates our corrector from generic isolated-word spelling correctors.
